Electricity primer

Voltage, current, ground, LED polarity, resistor role — no engineering degree.

Goal: Enough intuition to wire Module 0–3 labs without panic. Not a goal: Equations, circuit theory, or a physics exam.

Read this once before Lesson 0.3. Revisit when pull-ups or the night-light divider feel mysterious.

Guide

1. Three words you actually need

WordWater metaphorArduino meaning
VoltagePressureHow hard the electricity is “pushed” (Uno logic often ~5 V)
CurrentFlow rateHow much charge moves through a part (too much can fry an LED)
ResistanceA narrower pipeSomething that limits current (a resistor)

You do not need formulas for this course’s early labs. Remember the relationship in one sentence:

Note

For a simple path, more voltage or less resistance → more current. An LED wants some current, not a firehose.

Guide

2. A circuit must be a loop (closed path)

Electricity in these beginner projects needs a complete path:

  1. Out from a pin or from 5V
  2. Through parts (resistor, LED, …)
  3. Back to GND (ground)

If the path is broken — wire not clicked, LED backwards, GND forgotten — nothing happens. In Tinkercad a dark LED usually means an open path or wrong direction, not “the computer hates you.”

   [Uno pin 13] ──► resistor ──► LED ──► GND
         │                              ▲
         └──────── return path idea ────┘

Ground (GND) is the shared “0 V” reference and the place current returns. When lessons say “share GND,” they mean: every part that needs a return path should meet at the same GND idea (often one GND rail on a breadboard tied to Uno GND).

Guide

3. Why the resistor sits with the LED

An LED is not a tiny lightbulb that politely limits itself. Without a resistor, a real LED on 5 V can draw dangerous current and die (and stress the pin).

Habit for this course:

pin  →  resistor (often 220 Ω)  →  LED  →  GND
  • The resistor is in series (in the same single-file line), not a random side branch that does nothing.
  • In Tinkercad, set 220 Ω — not 220 kΩ (a thousand times larger; the LED may look “broken” because almost no current flows).
  • Simulation is forgiving; still use the resistor so real hardware habits are already correct.

Common beginner values on 5 V: 220 Ω, 330 Ω, or 1 kΩ.

4. LED polarity — one-way street

An LED is a diode: current through it mainly works in one direction.

NameRole in the simple storyTypical physical clue (real LED)
Anode (+)Faces toward the pin / resistorLonger lead
Cathode (−)Faces toward GNDShorter lead; flat spot on the plastic

If the LED is backwards: the sketch can be perfect and the LED stays dark. Always check polarity before rewriting code.

In Tinkercad: select the LED and use the inspector (side panel) — confirm which lead is anode vs cathode — then match the wiring above. Full click-path: how-to-tinkercad.md.

5. HIGH, LOW, and what your sketch is doing

When code does digitalWrite(ledPin, HIGH) on a Uno digital pin, that pin is driven toward the board’s logic supply (about 5 V) — “on pressure.” LOW ties the idea toward ground — “off / 0 V.”

So Blink is literally: connect pressure through resistor+LED to GND, then remove it, again and again.

You do not need to measure volts for Module 0. You do need the mental picture: pin → parts → GND.

6. Pull-ups — why a button can read HIGH when nobody presses

A floating pin (wired to nothing) can read random nonsense. A pull-up gently holds the pin at HIGH until something stronger pulls it down.

Arduino’s pinMode(buttonPin, INPUT_PULLUP) turns on a built-in pull-up. The usual beginner wiring:

Uno pin (INPUT_PULLUP) ── button ── GND
ButtonPin tends to read
Not pressedHIGH (pull-up wins)
PressedLOW (path to GND wins)

That “pressed = LOW” polarity surprises everyone once. Lesson 1.3 teaches it on purpose. If your LED logic feels inverted, check this table before flipping random if conditions — or print the raw reading with Serial (how-to).

(Pull-down is the mirror image: default LOW, press to 5V → HIGH. This course standardizes on pull-up + button to GND.)

7. Preview: voltage divider (for later night-light / LDR)

Two resistances in a line across 5V and GND make a middle voltage you can measure:

5V ── R1 ──●── R2 ── GND
           │
        analog pin reads here
  • If R1 and R2 change (or one is a light-dependent resistor), the voltage at ● moves.
  • Your sketch reads that with analogRead as a number (~0–1023 on Uno).
  • Module 3 uses this idea; a potentiometer is a ready-made adjustable divider and is the friendliest practice stand-in.

You do not need to calculate the voltage. You need: middle node goes to an analog pin; ends go to 5V and GND; never omit the series idea for LEDs on digital pins.

Guide

8. 5 V vs 3.3 V (future-you note)

  • Classic Arduino Uno labs in this course: think 5 V logic.
  • ESP32 (Module 8): many pins are 3.3 V. Feeding 5 V into a 3.3 V-only pin can damage it.

Ignore this until you leave Uno fundamentals — then re-read Module 7 power habits before mixing boards.

Guide

9. Tinkercad will not smoke — habits still matter

In simulation, wrong wiring often just fails silently (dark LED). On a desk:

  • Always series-resistor LEDs
  • Do not short 5V straight to GND
  • Use a data USB cable for Upload
  • Module 7 deepens “smoke-free” habits when hardware becomes required

10. Checkpoint (no board needed)

  1. Why does an LED need a path back to GND?
  2. What job does the 220 Ω resistor do?
  3. If the LED is flipped, can perfect Blink code still look “broken”?
  4. With INPUT_PULLUP and a button to GND, does “pressed” read HIGH or LOW?

Answers: (1) Current needs a closed loop / return. (2) Limit current. (3) Yes — polarity. (4) LOW.
